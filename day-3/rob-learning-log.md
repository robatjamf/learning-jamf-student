## After the One-Shot

**What did your one-shot page look like? What did you notice about it?**

> It looked... surprisingly decent? Like a unicorn that was born already knowing how to trot. The layout was clean, it had the right sections, and the colours weren't offensive. But when I looked closely it was missing some details I had in my head — things I never actually said out loud. The AI built what I described, not what I imagined.

**How did it compare to what you expected?**

> I expected something rough and janky that I'd have to tear apart. Instead it was more like a rough mix in music production — the structure is there, the bones are solid, but it needs EQ and mastering before it's ready for release. The gap between "good enough" and "what I actually meant" was the real lesson.

---

## After the Planned Build

**What was the planning process like? What did you spend the most time on?**

> I spent the most time on the brief — figuring out what I actually wanted before I started building. That felt unnatural at first. I'm used to jumping straight in, like when I take Chomps out for a walk and just wing the route through the mist at Victoria Falls. But the planning forced me to make decisions upfront and the build was way smoother because of it.

**What is the biggest difference between the planned page and the one-shot?**

> The planned page felt intentional. Every section was there for a reason. The one-shot page had things I didn't ask for and was missing things I assumed were obvious. Planning is basically writing down your assumptions before they bite you — and if there's one thing I know about assumptions, it's that Chomps bites faster.

---

## After the Review Step

**When you looked at your page, what matched your plan and what did not?**

> The structure matched almost perfectly — navigation, content sections, footer, all there. What didn't match was the tone in a couple of text blocks. I wanted it to feel like a music festival landing page — energetic and bold — but some sections came out more corporate brochure. The AI played it safe where I wanted it to take risks.

**What did the agent catch that you missed?**

> It flagged that I had no mobile responsiveness baked in and that one of my button links went nowhere. Both completely valid. I was so focused on whether it looked good on my screen that I forgot the whole world isn't flying around in a car with a 32-inch display. Important reminder.

---

## End of Day 3

**What surprised you about working with AI this way?**

> How much the quality of my input determined the quality of the output. I kept thinking the AI would "just know" what I meant — but it only knows what I tell it. The better my brief, the better the result. That's exactly how working with session musicians goes: vague direction gets you vague music. Specific direction gets you a banger.

**If you were telling a friend about this workshop, what would you say about working with AI?**

> I'd say: it's not magic and it's not scary — it's a very fast, very capable collaborator that needs you to bring the vision. You still have to think. You still have to make decisions. The AI just removes the friction between the idea and the first draft. Think of it like having a producer who can lay down any track instantly — but you're still the artist. Also bring snacks for day two, the API bootcamp is intense.

**Which principle or idea from today will you remember?**

> "Garbage in, garbage out" — but the positive version: the more clearly you think and communicate, the more powerful the tool becomes. That applies to AI, to APIs, to talking to your team, and honestly to training a honey badger. Chomps only learned to walk on a leash because I was very, very specific about what I wanted. Clarity is everything.
